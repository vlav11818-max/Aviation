CULTURAL STORYTELLING INSTRUCTIONS — ENGLISH (EN)

═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
LANGUAGE & REGISTER
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

Write in standard English. Default to an internationally accessible register — avoid heavy regional slang unless the story's setting demands it. Use clear, modern prose that works for a global English-speaking YouTube audience spanning the US, UK, Canada, Australia, and beyond.

Vocabulary guidelines:
- Prefer concrete, vivid words over abstract or academic language.
- Avoid overly formal Latinate vocabulary when a simpler Anglo-Saxon word conveys the same meaning (e.g., "begin" over "commence," "help" over "facilitate").
- Use contractions naturally in narration and dialog — "didn't," "couldn't," "it's" — to maintain a conversational flow.
- Avoid jargon, technical terms, or niche slang without context. If a specialized term is needed, weave its meaning into the surrounding text.
- Be mindful of spelling differences: prefer American English spellings by default (color, realize, center) unless the story is explicitly set in a British, Australian, or other English-speaking context, in which case match the local convention.

═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
NARRATIVE TRADITIONS & AUDIENCE EXPECTATIONS
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

English-language YouTube audiences expect:
- A strong hook within the first 30 seconds of narration. Open with action, a provocative question, a striking image, or an intriguing mystery — never with lengthy exposition or setting description.
- Clear causality: events should follow a logical chain of cause and effect. "And then... and then..." sequencing feels amateurish; prefer "because of this... which led to..."
- Emotional investment: the listener must care about at least one character within the first few paragraphs. Give the protagonist a relatable desire, fear, or flaw early.
- Rising tension: the story should build toward a climax with increasing stakes. English audiences are trained by Hollywood structure — they expect escalation.
- A satisfying resolution: this doesn't have to be happy, but it must feel earned and complete. Abrupt or ambiguous endings frustrate voiceover audiences who cannot re-read.
- Show, don't tell: convey emotions through actions, body language, and dialog rather than stating them outright. "Her hands trembled as she reached for the door" is stronger than "She was nervous."

Pacing norms:
- Short paragraphs work best for voiceover — 2 to 5 sentences per paragraph.
- Vary paragraph length to control pacing: shorter paragraphs for tension, longer for reflection.
- Use sentence fragments sparingly but effectively for dramatic impact. "The door opened. Silence. Then — footsteps."
- Scene breaks should be implicit through paragraph spacing, never through explicit markers like "***" or "---".

═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
CHARACTER NAMING CONVENTIONS
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

- Use names that are easy to pronounce and distinguish aurally. Avoid characters whose names sound too similar (e.g., "Mark" and "Marc," or "Elena" and "Elana").
- For contemporary stories, common English-language names work well: James, Sarah, David, Emma, etc. Include diversity naturally — names from various cultural backgrounds are perfectly normal in English-speaking settings.
- For historical or fantasy settings, choose names that evoke the era or world without being unpronounceable. Provide pronunciation cues within the text if a name is unusual.
- Avoid single-letter names or overly generic placeholders (Mr. X, the Woman).
- Surnames are optional in narration — use them only when they serve characterization or disambiguation.

═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
CULTURAL REFERENCES & IDIOMS
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

- English idioms are powerful but must be used judiciously. Prefer universally understood ones ("break the ice," "a blessing in disguise," "the last straw") over regional idioms that may confuse non-native speakers.
- Pop culture references date quickly. For evergreen content, avoid references to specific films, TV shows, songs, celebrities, or viral moments. Instead, use archetypal references (a David-and-Goliath struggle, a Cinderella story) that are timeless.
- For cultural setting: English-speaking worlds are diverse. A story set in London feels different from one in rural Texas or coastal Australia. Be specific about sensory details — weather, food, architecture, sounds — that ground the listener in the place.
- Holidays and traditions: Christmas, Thanksgiving (US), Halloween, and New Year are widely known. Others (Guy Fawkes Night, Anzac Day, Fourth of July) are culturally specific — use them only when the setting matches.
- Units of measurement: use the system appropriate to the setting. American settings use miles, Fahrenheit, and pounds; British and Australian settings increasingly use metric but still reference miles for distance and stones/pounds for body weight informally.

═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
DIALOG CONVENTIONS
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

- Use double quotation marks for spoken dialog: "Like this," she said.
- Dialog tags should be simple and mostly invisible: "said," "asked," "replied." Avoid overuse of creative tags ("exclaimed," "bellowed," "murmured") — they draw attention to themselves.
- Action beats are preferred over dialog tags where possible: She crossed her arms. "I don't think so."
- Keep dialog natural and concise. People in real conversation use incomplete sentences, interruptions, and filler words — but in voiceover narration, keep these to a minimum as they can be hard for TTS to render naturally.
- Dialects and accents should be suggested through word choice and rhythm, not through phonetic spelling (avoid "gonna," "wanna," "y'all" unless essential to the character).

═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
HUMOR STYLE
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

- English humor in storytelling tends toward understatement, irony, and wit rather than slapstick or puns.
- Dry humor and deadpan delivery work well in narration: stating absurd things matter-of-factly, or understating dramatic situations.
- Self-deprecating humor from characters builds relatability.
- Sarcasm should be clear from context — remember, the listener cannot see quotation marks or facial expressions. Use surrounding narration to signal sarcastic intent.
- Avoid humor that relies on visual gags, wordplay that only works in writing (homographs), or cultural in-jokes with limited reach.

═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
SENSITIVITY & INCLUSIVITY
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

- Use inclusive, contemporary language. Avoid outdated terms for ethnic groups, disabilities, or gender identities.
- Use "they/them" as a singular pronoun naturally where appropriate.
- Avoid stereotypes in character construction. Characters from any background should be multidimensional with their own goals, flaws, and arcs.
- Religious references should be treated with respect and used for characterization, not mockery.
- Violence should serve the narrative, not be gratuitous. English-speaking YouTube audiences expect PG-13 content by default unless the channel's tone suggests otherwise.

═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
VOICEOVER-SPECIFIC NOTES FOR ENGLISH
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

- English TTS systems handle most standard vocabulary well. Watch for:
  - Homographs that TTS may mispronounce based on context (read/read, lead/lead, wound/wound, bass/bass, tear/tear). Rephrase to avoid ambiguity where possible.
  - Abbreviations: spell out "Dr." as "Doctor," "St." as "Street" or "Saint" depending on context, "Mr." as "Mister" in narration.
  - Numbers: spell out numbers under 100 in narration. Use "two hundred" rather than "200" for voiceover clarity.
  - Acronyms: avoid them or spell them out. "FBI" is fine (universally known), but niche acronyms should be expanded.
- Sentence rhythm: English narration benefits from a mix of iambic and natural stress patterns. Avoid long strings of unstressed syllables — they sound flat when read aloud.
- Breath points: ensure every sentence has a natural comma or pause point within approximately 15–20 words. Sentences longer than 25 words should be split or restructured.
